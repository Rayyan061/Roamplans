Travel Insurance Mistakes to Avoid Before and During Your Trip

✓ Before buying▣ During the trip↗ Claim mistakes

Travel insurance mistakes often happen long before a traveler needs to make a claim. When I review insurance for a trip ↗, I try to find the weak points first: what I could lose, what my existing insurance already covers, what the new policy excludes, whether my activities fit, and what I would actually need to do if something went wrong.

I also avoid treating travel insurance as a simple checkout product. Two policies with similar prices and benefit names can work very differently because each policy sets its own limits, covered situations, and exclusions. NAIC's June 2026 guidance specifically advises travelers to ask exactly what a policy covers before buying.

Most mistakes are preventable. The goal is not to buy every possible benefit. It is to understand the policy well enough that you do not discover an important gap after your money, health, or trip plans are already affected.

!
Travel Note

Insurance terms vary by policy, insurer, state or country of residence, destination, and trip. Use these mistakes as a practical review checklist, but always rely on your own policy wording for the final coverage rules.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓

  • ✓
    Calculate your actual non-refundable trip cost before deciding how much cancellation protection matters.
  • ✓
    Check existing health, credit-card, and other insurance before buying duplicate protection.
  • ✓
    Do not assume your normal health insurance works abroad.
  • ✓
    Medical treatment and medical evacuation are different risks. The State Department recommends checking both before international travel.
  • ✓
    Read exclusions as carefully as benefits.
  • ✓
    Pre-existing-condition rules should be checked before buying, not after a medical problem affects the trip.
  • ✓
    Confirm that every important activity is actually covered.
  • ✓
    Do not assume Cancel for Any Reason means a full refund for any cancellation.
  • ✓
    Make sure the policy covers every destination and the full length of your trip.
  • ✓

  • ✓

  • ✓
    During a problem, document what happened while evidence is still easy to obtain.

    Do not hide refunds, credits, or other reimbursements when making a claim.

Mistake 1: Buying Travel Insurance Only Because It Is Cheap

Price matters, but it should not be the first filter.

A cheap policy can be suitable if it covers the risks that matter to your trip.

It becomes poor value when the lower price comes with a gap involving:

  • ✓
    Medical care
  • ✓
    Evacuation
  • ✓
    Main cancellation risk
  • ✓
    Important activity
  • ✓
    Trip duration

NAIC says travel-insurance cost depends on what the policy includes and other factors such as destination, age, and season. Its current consumer guidance gives a broad typical range of about 5%–10% of trip price, but that percentage does not tell you whether a particular policy fits your trip.

A Better Approach

First decide what protection you actually need.

Then compare prices among policies that pass those requirements.

The detailed selection process belongs in How to Choose the Best Travel Insurance for Your Trip.

For this article, remember one rule:

Cheap is useful only when the coverage still fits.

Mistake 2: Assuming the Most Expensive Policy Must Be Best

Travelers can make the opposite mistake too.

A higher premium can come with:

  • ✓
    Larger limits
  • ✓
    More benefits
  • ✓
    Extra activity coverage
  • ✓
    Additional services

But you may not need all of them.

Suppose you are taking a short city trip with:

  • ✓
    Refundable accommodation
  • ✓
    No adventure activities
  • ✓
    Strong existing international health protection

Paying much more for benefits designed around extreme sports or unusually high cancellation exposure may add little practical value.

A Better Approach

Compare the policy with your own risk profile rather than using price as a quality score.

The best fit may be:

  • ✓
    Cheapest suitable option
  • ✓
    Mid-priced option
  • ✓
    Higher-priced option

depending on what your trip actually requires.

Mistake 3: Buying Before Calculating What You Could Really Lose

Travelers often say:

“My trip costs $6,000.”

That does not necessarily mean $6,000 is at risk.

Maybe:

  • ✓
    Hotel is refundable
  • ✓
    Airline ticket is non-refundable
  • ✓
    Tour provides a partial refund
  • ✓
    Train ticket cannot be changed

Before comparing cancellation protection, calculate the amount you could genuinely lose.

Example

BookingPaidRefundableAmount at Risk
Flight$1,500No$1,500
Hotel$2,000Yes$0
Tour$90050%$450
Train$200No$200

Total trip spending:

$4,600

Potential non-refundable exposure:

$2,150

Those are very different numbers.

A Better Approach

Separate:

total trip price

from

non-refundable financial exposure.

That gives you a much better starting point for insurance decisions.

Mistake 4: Assuming Every Cancellation Is Covered

Seeing:

Trip Cancellation — 100%

can create false confidence.

Standard cancellation protection normally depends on the reason meeting the policy's covered conditions.

NAIC's current guidance gives examples such as certain illnesses or injuries, hospitalization or death of a qualifying family member, severe weather, transportation disruption, and some natural disasters, but it also emphasizes that each policy defines what it will and will not cover.

Changing your mind is not the same as experiencing a covered event.

A Better Approach

Ask:

What are the three most realistic reasons I might cancel this trip?

Then search the policy for each one.

For the full rules, use Trip Cancellation Insurance Guide: What Is Covered and What Is Not?

Mistake 5: Assuming Cancel for Any Reason Means a Full Refund

The name Cancel for Any Reason, or CFAR, sounds almost unlimited.

It is not.

CFAR typically has:

  • ✓
    Purchase deadlines
  • ✓
    Eligibility requirements
  • ✓
    Cancellation timing rules
  • ✓
    Partial reimbursement

NAIC's current travel-insurance guidance describes CFAR as broader than standard cancellation coverage but generally reimbursing only part of the trip price rather than providing an automatic full refund.

A Better Approach

Before paying extra for CFAR, check:

  • ✓
    Percentage reimbursed
  • ✓
    Purchase deadline
  • ✓
    How much trip cost must be insured
  • ✓
    How early you must cancel
  • ✓
    Extra premium

Buy it only if that broader flexibility solves a risk that matters to you.

Mistake 6: Assuming Your Normal Health Insurance Works Abroad

This is one of the biggest mistakes an international traveler can make.

Your normal health plan may:

  • ✓
    Cover overseas emergencies
  • ✓
    Provide limited overseas protection
  • ✓
    Require reimbursement later
  • ✓
    Provide little useful overseas coverage

The State Department recommends checking directly with your health insurer about emergency and routine care abroad and considering short-term travel medical insurance when existing protection is inadequate.

A Better Approach

Before buying additional medical protection, ask your normal insurer:

  • ✓
    Does my policy work in this country?
  • ✓
    Does it cover emergency treatment?
  • ✓
    Does it cover hospitalization?
  • ✓
    Will I need to pay upfront?
  • ✓
    How are international claims handled?
  • ✓
    Is medical evacuation included?

Do not settle for:

“Yes, you're covered abroad.”

Understand how that coverage actually works.

Mistake 7: Assuming Medicare Follows You Worldwide

For U.S. travelers who rely on Medicare, overseas medical protection deserves separate attention.

The State Department states that Medicare and Medicaid do not pay for medical care outside the United States in its international travel insurance guidance and advises travelers to examine additional protection.

Specific Medicare exceptions and supplemental-plan benefits can exist, so travelers should check their exact coverage.

A Better Approach

If Medicare-related coverage is relevant to you:

  • ✓
    Check your exact Medicare situation.
  • ✓
    Check any Medigap or Medicare Advantage benefits.
  • ✓
    Identify the remaining international medical gap.
  • ✓
    Compare travel medical insurance against that gap.

For the deeper medical discussion, use Travel Medical Insurance Guide for International Travelers.

Mistake 8: Forgetting About Medical Evacuation

A traveler can have medical treatment coverage but weak evacuation protection.

Those are different risks.

The State Department says some health plans cover typical medical costs abroad while most do not pay to bring a traveler back to the United States by air ambulance. It currently estimates that such evacuation can cost about $20,000–$200,000, depending on location and medical condition.

A Better Approach

Check medical evacuation separately.

Ask:

  • ✓
    What is the limit?
  • ✓
    Who decides evacuation is medically necessary?
  • ✓
    Where would I be transported?
  • ✓
    Does the insurer need to authorize it?
  • ✓
    Does the benefit fit a remote destination?

The issue becomes especially important in areas with limited medical care. The State Department strongly recommends medical evacuation coverage for higher-risk or medically limited destinations.

Mistake 9: Assuming Medical Evacuation Means “Fly Me Home”

Medical evacuation does not necessarily mean you can choose to fly directly to your preferred hospital at home.

The policy may instead focus on medically necessary transportation to an appropriate medical facility.

The important terms can include:

  • ✓
    Medical necessity
  • ✓
    Appropriate facility
  • ✓
    Authorization
  • ✓
    Repatriation

A Better Approach

Read the evacuation wording before departure.

Do not wait until an emergency to discover who makes the transportation decision.

Mistake 10: Reading the Benefits but Skipping the Exclusions

A benefit table tells you what protection may exist.

The exclusions tell you when it may not.

NAIC's April 2026 travel-insurance guidance identifies common exclusion areas that can include:

  • ✓
    Pre-existing health conditions
  • ✓
    Pandemics
  • ✓
    Civil or political unrest
  • ✓
    Pregnancy and childbirth
  • ✓
    Certain risky activities

while emphasizing that every policy differs.

A Better Approach

For each major benefit you care about, ask:

What could stop this coverage from applying?

Search the policy for the exclusions connected to your:

  • ✓
    Health
  • ✓
    Destination
  • ✓
    Activities
  • ✓
    Cancellation concerns

You do not need to memorize the entire contract.

You do need to understand exclusions that directly affect your trip.

Mistake 11: Ignoring Pre-Existing-Condition Rules Until Something Happens

A traveler may see a policy with strong medical and cancellation benefits and assume an existing health problem is automatically covered.

That can be risky.

NAIC specifically advises travelers to check whether pre-existing medical conditions are excluded before relying on travel medical protection.

Some policies can have waiver provisions under specific conditions.

Those rules need to be checked before the trip.

A Better Approach

If a pre-existing condition could matter:

  • ✓
    Find the policy definition
  • ✓
    Check any look-back period
  • ✓
    Check waiver eligibility
  • ✓
    Check purchase timing requirements
  • ✓
    Ask the insurer if wording is unclear

Do not rely on your personal definition of what counts as pre-existing.

Mistake 12: Waiting Too Long to Compare Insurance

Some travelers wait until the night before departure.

That can limit options.

A problem can also become known before insurance is purchased.

Insurance is designed around uncertain future events, not losses that are already happening or clearly known.

NAIC's current consumer guidance emphasizes reviewing insurance before travel and checking the policy's actual covered situations and limitations before purchase.

A Better Approach

Compare appropriate policies once you begin making meaningful non-refundable trip payments, especially when time-sensitive benefits are important.

Do not confuse:

buy early

with

buy immediately without reading.

You still need to compare.

Mistake 13: Automatically Buying the Policy Offered at Checkout

Airlines, cruises, booking sites, and travel companies often offer protection during checkout.

That option may be suitable.

But its location beside the Pay Now button does not make it automatically appropriate for your whole trip.

NAIC explains that travel protection packages can contain insurance alongside non-insurance services or products.

A Better Approach

Before selecting:

Add Travel Protection

check:

  • ✓
    What product is being sold?
  • ✓
    Who provides the insurance?
  • ✓
    What benefits are included?
  • ✓
    What is excluded?
  • ✓
    Does it cover the rest of the trip or only part of the booking?

Then compare it with other appropriate options.

Mistake 14: Confusing a Cancellation Waiver With Travel Insurance

A supplier may offer a cancellation waiver.

That is not necessarily the same as a comprehensive travel insurance policy.

NAIC distinguishes travel insurance from non-insurance products that can be bundled with travel protection.

A waiver may address particular supplier cancellation charges without providing the same protection as insurance for:

  • ✓
    Medical treatment
  • ✓
    Medical evacuation
  • ✓
    Baggage
  • ✓
    Other travel losses

A Better Approach

Identify exactly what you are purchasing.

Ask:

Is this insurance, a supplier waiver, an assistance service, or a bundle?

Mistake 17: Assuming “Adventure Activities Covered” Means Your Activity Is Covered

This is a major mistake for active trips.

The State Department advises travelers to make sure their insurance covers the activities they plan to participate in, while NAIC warns that dangerous activities can be excluded from medical evacuation or other protection.

The words:

sports included

are not enough.

A Better Approach

Describe the actual activity.

Instead of:

Hiking

check:

Guided trek reaching 4,800 meters.

Instead of:

Scuba

check:

Recreational scuba to 30 meters with my certification level.

Mistake 19: Assuming Medical Coverage Automatically Covers an Adventure Injury

You may see:

Emergency Medical — $250,000

and assume any injury qualifies.

But if the injury happens during an excluded activity, the medical benefit may not work as expected.

The State Department's adventure guidance specifically advises travelers to make sure insurance covers the activities they plan to participate in and any special medical risks they anticipate.

A Better Approach

For every important adventure activity, check three things separately:

  • ✓
    Activity itself
  • ✓
    Medical treatment after an activity-related injury
  • ✓
    Evacuation or rescue after the activity

Do not assume one answer covers all three.

Mistake 22: Ignoring Per-Person, Per-Trip, and Per-Policy Limits

A policy may advertise:

Travel Delay — $1,000

  • ✓

A Better Approach

Baggage coverage is useful.

But it may not be the largest risk on your trip.

A Better Approach

financially difficult

to

financially manageable.

My Rule Before Buying Travel Insurance

I try not to ask:

“Is this a good travel insurance policy?”

That question is too broad.

I ask:

“What are the biggest financial risks on this trip, which of them am I trying to transfer to the insurer, and what policy wording could stop that protection from working?”

That question catches many insurance mistakes before they become expensive ones.

Mistake 26: Not Rechecking the Policy After Your Trip Changes

Buying insurance is not always the final insurance task.

Trips can change after purchase.

You might:

  • ✓
    Add another country
  • ✓
    Extend the trip
  • ✓
    Add an expensive tour
  • ✓
    Book a cruise
  • ✓
    Add a higher-risk activity
  • ✓
    Increase non-refundable trip costs

If the trip you eventually take is materially different from the trip you originally insured, check whether the insurer needs updated information.

A Better Approach

Whenever you make an important change, ask:

Does this change affect my policy dates, destinations, insured trip cost, activities, or eligibility?

Do not assume the original policy automatically adjusts itself.

Mistake 35: Traveling Without Access to Emergency Funds Because You Have Insurance

Travel insurance does not always mean you can receive care or services without paying anything first.

In some destinations or situations, travelers may need access to funds before reimbursement arrangements are completed.

A Better Approach

  • ✓
    Payment card
  • ✓
    Backup payment method
  • ✓
    Emergency funds

Mistake 36: Paying for a Major Medical Evacuation Without Contacting the Insurer When Possible

NAIC also advises travelers to ask whether insurer approval is required before receiving medical care and explains that evacuation can involve transport to the nearest hospital capable of providing appropriate treatment.

Get necessary emergency help first.

  • ✓

  • ✓
    Hospital transfer
  • ✓
    Medical flight
  • ✓

Mistake 37: Delaying Emergency Treatment Just to Contact the Insurer

If I had to reduce this entire guide to five mistakes, I would choose these.

1. Buying Without Understanding the Main Exclusions

Benefits tell you what may be protected.

2. Assuming Existing Health Insurance Works Internationally

Confirm it directly before departure.

The State Department recommends travelers check their overseas medical protection and consider travel medical and evacuation insurance where appropriate.

3. Forgetting Medical Evacuation

This becomes especially important when traveling to remote locations or places with limited medical care.

Before leaving home, I would ask:

Policy

Do I have the correct policy and dates?

Travelers

Is everyone who expects coverage properly included?

Destination

Does the policy fit my full itinerary?

Medical

Do I understand my overseas medical coverage?

Evacuation

Do I know how emergency evacuation works?

Activities

Are the main activities of this trip covered?

Emergency Contact

Can I reach the insurer without searching through hundreds of emails?

Documents

Can I access the policy offline?

If I can answer all eight confidently, I am much less likely to discover an obvious insurance problem during the trip.
