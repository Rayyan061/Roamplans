Customs and Immigration Explained for International Travelers

✦ Immigration checks ✓ Customs declarations ↕ Arrival process

Arriving in another country can feel confusing the first time. You leave the aircraft, follow signs ↗ for arrivals, reach passport control, collect your baggage, and then pass through customs. Each stage has a different purpose, even though travelers often use the words customs and immigration as if they mean the same thing.

No, customs and immigration are not the same. Immigration mainly checks you, your identity, and your permission to enter. Customs mainly checks the goods and belongings you are bringing into the country. At many international airports, immigration comes first, followed by baggage collection and then customs.

I prepare for both before flying. I keep my passport and entry documents easy to reach, save my first accommodation address offline, and check what needs to be declared before packing. This prevents me from trying to research food, medicines, cash, or other restricted items while standing in an airport queue.

The exact process differs by country and airport, so travelers should check current destination rules before departure. Review your passport requirements and visa requirements before relying on a general airport guide. For U.S. travelers going abroad, the U.S. Department of State recommends reviewing destination-specific entry requirements before travel.

!
Travel Note

Immigration, customs, agricultural, currency, and declaration rules vary by country and can change. Use the destination government's official immigration and customs information before traveling.

What Is Immigration?

Immigration is the part of border control that deals with people entering or leaving a country.

At international arrival, an immigration or border officer may check whether you are legally allowed to enter.

Depending on the country and your situation, this can involve your passport, visa, eVisa, ETA, entry record, travel purpose, length of stay, and other supporting information.

For foreign visitors entering the United States, U.S. Customs and Border Protection handles admission at the border. CBP explains that travelers seeking entry are inspected to establish whether they are admissible under U.S. law.

Other countries use their own immigration or border agencies, but the basic goal is similar: confirm who you are and whether your trip meets the country's entry rules.

What Is Customs?

Customs is mainly concerned with what crosses the border with you.

That can include personal belongings, items bought abroad, gifts, food, alcohol, tobacco, medicines, plants, animal products, commercial goods, and large amounts of currency.

Customs officers may decide whether an item can enter, whether it needs to be declared, and whether any tax or duty applies.

For travelers entering the United States, CBP's declaration process asks for information about merchandise and agricultural products being brought into the country.

So if you want one simple way to separate the two:

Immigration asks: Are you allowed to enter?

Customs asks: What are you bringing in?

What Happens After an International Flight Lands?

The exact order varies, but a common international arrival follows this pattern:

Aircraft → arrivals corridor → immigration/passport control → baggage claim → customs → arrivals hall

Some airports change the order slightly or combine parts of the process.

If you have a connecting international or domestic flight, you may also need to recheck baggage and pass through security again after border formalities.

Knowing the basic sequence helps because you can keep the right documents available instead of packing them inside checked baggage.

Step 1: Follow Signs for Arrivals or Passport Control

After leaving the aircraft, follow the airport signs for:

Arrivals, Immigration, Passport Control, Border Control, or Connections.

Do not automatically follow other passengers. Some may be transferring to another international flight and using a different route through the airport.

If your connection requires you to enter the country, collect bags, or change airports, you may need to complete normal immigration procedures even though the country is not your final destination.

This is one reason transit requirements should be checked before departure.

Step 2: Join the Correct Immigration Line

Large international airports often divide passengers into different queues.

The categories can vary by country and may include citizens, residents, foreign visitors, electronic-gate users, families, or travelers enrolled in trusted-traveler programs.

Read the signs before joining a line.

A U.S. citizen returning to the United States and a foreign visitor arriving on a tourist visa may use different processing options even though they arrived on the same aircraft.

Some airports also use electronic gates or biometric processing for eligible travelers.

What Does an Immigration Officer Check?

The officer normally starts with your travel document.

That may involve checking your identity, passport validity, visa or travel authorization, travel history, permitted activities, and whether you appear to meet the country's entry conditions.

For visitors entering the United States, CBP describes the first border examination as primary inspection. Travelers may then be admitted or referred for further inspection if additional checks are needed.

Your destination may use different names, but the idea is similar.

The first check usually decides whether your entry is straightforward or whether more information is required.

What Questions Can Immigration Ask?

First-time travelers sometimes worry that immigration questions mean there is a problem.

In many cases, routine questions are simply part of confirming the purpose and conditions of a trip.

You may be asked where you are staying, why you are visiting, how long you plan to remain, when you will leave, whether you are visiting someone, or where you are traveling next.

Keep your answers short, accurate, and consistent with your travel documents.

If you are visiting for tourism, you should be able to explain your basic travel plan without creating a long story.

For example:

Officer: Why are you visiting?

Traveler: Tourism. I am visiting New York for seven days.

That is usually more useful than giving a long explanation that was never requested.

Keep Your First Accommodation Details Ready

Border officers or arrival forms may ask where you will stay.

Keep your first accommodation information easy to access.

I save the hotel name and address offline because international mobile data may not work immediately after landing.

If you are staying with family or friends, keep their address and contact details available where required.

This does not mean every immigration officer will ask to see a hotel reservation. It means you are ready if the information is requested.

Can Immigration Ask for Your Return or Onward Ticket?

Yes, depending on the destination and your entry status.

Some countries expect visitors to show that they plan to leave within their permitted stay.

For example, current U.S. State Department information for Panama lists a return ticket among its entry requirements for U.S. travelers, along with passport validity and proof of funds.

The requirement is not universal.

Check your destination rather than carrying an onward ticket because someone online said every country asks for one.

Can Immigration Ask for Proof of Funds?

Some countries may ask travelers to show that they can support themselves during the visit.

Again, this is destination-specific.

Panama's current entry information for U.S. travelers, for example, lists proof of funds along with other entry requirements.

Possible evidence depends on the country's rules.

Do not carry sensitive financial documents unless there is a reason to do so. Check what the destination actually requires before traveling.

Does Having a Visa Guarantee Entry?

No.

A visa or other authorization can allow you to travel to the border and request entry, but the border authority can still check whether you meet the conditions.

That is why your trip purpose should match the permission you are using.

Someone arriving as a tourist should not describe plans that clearly involve unauthorized employment.

The same idea applies to visa-free travelers. Visa exemption removes the normal visa requirement for an eligible visit; it does not remove immigration control.

What Is Secondary Inspection?

Sometimes a traveler is sent for additional border checks after the first inspection.

In the United States, CBP refers to this as secondary i nspection. CBP's inspection process allows officers to refer travelers for additional examination when more information or checks are needed.

A referral does not by itself prove that the traveler has committed an offense.

Additional inspection can happen because officers need more time to check documents, travel information, admissibility, baggage, or other details.

If you are referred, follow the officer's instructions and answer questions accurately.

Do not make jokes about weapons, drugs, immigration status, or security.

What Happens After Passport Control?

Once immigration is complete, travelers at many airports move to baggage claim.

Collect your checked luggage and make sure you have the correct bags before continuing.

Keep your baggage tags until you are outside the controlled arrival area. They can help if a suitcase is missing or if airport staff need to confirm ownership.

After collecting baggage, you normally continue toward customs.

That is where the focus moves from you to what you are carrying.

What Is a Customs Declaration?

A customs declaration tells border authorities about goods or items you are bringing into the country.

The exact questions vary.

For entry into the United States, CBP Form 6059B gathers information about the traveler and items being brought into the country, including purchased merchandise and agricultural products.

Some countries still use paper forms in some situations.

Others use:

online forms, mobile apps, airport kiosks, electronic gates, or verbal declarations.

Do not assume there will always be a paper card on the aircraft.

Check the current destination process before departure.

What Does It Mean to Declare Something?

Declaring something means telling customs that you are carrying it.

It does not automatically mean the item is banned.

It does not automatically mean you will pay a fine.

An officer may inspect it, decide whether it can enter, calculate duty where applicable, or allow you to continue.

If you are unsure whether something should be declared, declaring it is generally safer than intentionally hiding it.

For U.S. entry, CBP requires travelers to declare agricultural products and provides separate rules for purchases, gifts, alcohol, tobacco, and other items.

Food and Agricultural Products Need Special Attention

Food is one of the easiest areas for travelers to get wrong.

Something that looks harmless in a supermarket can still be restricted when carried across an international border.

For travelers entering the United States, CBP says agricultural products such as meats, fruits, vegetables, plants, seeds, soil, animals, and plant or animal products must be declared.

Some products may then be allowed after inspection, while others may be prohibited or restricted.

The safest habit is simple:

If you are carrying food or an agricultural product, check the destination's customs rules before packing it.

Do not rely on the fact that the food is sealed, commercially packaged, or bought at an airport.

Purchases and Gifts Can Still Need to Be Declared

Travelers sometimes assume that customs declarations apply only to expensive commercial goods.

That is not always true.

For travelers returning to the United States, CBP says items bought abroad and gifts received abroad can fall within customs declaration requirements.

This can include souvenirs, clothing, electronics, jewelry, gifts, and other purchases.

Whether duty is owed is a separate question from whether an item needs to be declared.

Do not hide a purchase because you think it may be below a duty-free allowance.

Declare according to the rules, then let customs determine whether any duty applies.

What About Carrying Cash Across a Border?

Countries can have reporting rules for large amounts of cash or other monetary instruments.

For the United States, there is no general ban on carrying more than $10,000, but amounts over $10,000 in U.S. or foreign currency or qualifyin g monetary instruments must be reported when entering or leaving the country.

This is a good example of why travelers should separate:

prohibited

from

reportable.

Something can be legal to carry while still requiring a declaration.

Other countries set their own thresholds and reporting systems, so check the rules for both departure and arrival countries if traveling with a large amount of money.

What Are the Red and Green Customs Channels?

Some international airports use separate customs channels after baggage claim.

The most common system is:

Green channel: nothing to declare

Red channel: goods or cash to declare

The UK currently uses this system at many ports and airports. Travelers with goods within their allowances and no banned or restricted items can normally use the green channel. Travelers who have goods or cash to declare use the red channel or a red-point phone where available.

The European Union also recognizes going through a green "nothing to declare" channel as a form of customs declaration.

Does Green Mean You Cannot Be Checked?

No.

Choosing the green channel means you are declaring that you have nothing requiring a separate declaration under that country's rules.

Customs officers can still stop travelers and inspect baggage. UK Border Force guidance specifically states that travelers and their baggage can be checked even when passing through customs.

Do not choose the green channel because the red line looks longer.

Choose it only if your belongings meet the rules.

What Is a Duty-Free Allowance?

A duty-free allowance lets an eligible traveler bring a certain value or quantity of personal goods into a country without paying customs duty.

This does not mean everything bought at a "duty-free" airport shop automatically enters another country without tax.

The destination's customs rules still apply.

For travelers returning to the United States, CBP currently uses different personal exemptions depending on factors such as where the traveler has been. Common exemption levels include $200, $800, or $1,600, subject to eligibility requirements and limits on certain products.

Personal Allowance vs Declaration

These are different ideas.

A customs declaration answers:

What am I bringing into the country?

A duty-free allowance answers:

How much of it can enter without duty or tax?

You may need to declare an item even when no duty is eventually owed.

Let customs make that decision according to the destination's rules.

Alcohol and Customs

Alcohol rules differ by country, age, destination, and quantity.

For travelers entering the United States, CBP currently says travelers age 21 or older can generally include one liter of alcohol in their duty-free exemption, although different allowances can apply to travelers arriving from certain Caribbean locations or the U.S. Virgin Islands. Additional alcohol may be allowed but can be subject to duty and federal excise tax.

State alcohol rules can also apply after federal customs rules are met.

Tobacco Products and Customs

Tobacco products also have quantity limits.

For a returning U.S. resident who qualifies for the standard $800 exemption, CBP currently states that the exemption can include up to 200 cigarettes and 100 cigars, subject to the applicable conditions.

The exact allowance can change depending on the exemption category and where you traveled.

If you carry more than the permitted duty-free amount, declare it.

Do not split goods between bags to make the quantity look smaller.

Customs rules apply to what the traveler is actually importing.

Traveling Internationally With Medication

Medication requires extra care because immigration, customs, drug-control, and health rules can all apply.

A medicine that is legally prescribed at home may be controlled, restricted, or unapproved somewhere else.

For travelers bringing medication into the United States, CBP currently recommends carrying a valid prescription or doctor's note written in English.

FDA guidance also recommends keeping prescription medicine in its original container where possible and carrying only personal-use quantities, with roughly a 90-day supply used as a general rule of thumb in its traveler guidance.

Keep Medication Clearly Identified

For prescription medicine, consider carrying:

Do this before departure rather than assuming a pharmacy label will be accepted in every country.

Controlled Medicines Need Extra Checking

Some prescription medicines are also controlled substances.

FDA states that the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration may decide whether a controlled substance can be imported for personal use when DEA rules apply.

Other countries have their own controlled-drug laws.

If you take medicines such as strong pain medication, certain sleeping medicines, ADHD medication, anxiety medication, or other controlled drugs, check the destination's official health, customs, embassy, or medicines authority.

Do not rely only on the fact that you have a legal prescription at home.

Buying Medicine Abroad and Bringing It Home

This requires separate checking.

For U.S. citizens, FDA says that in most cases importing foreign drugs for personal use is illegal when those products are not approved for use and sale in the United States, although limited personal-importation situations may be considered under specific conditions.

So do not assume:

I bought it legally overseas, so I can bring it home.

The destination and return-country laws both matter.

If You Are Unsure, Declare the Item

A declaration does not mean you are admitting that you broke a rule.

You are telling customs what you have and allowing the officer to decide what happens next.

Australian Border Force, for example, specifically tells travelers they will not be penalized under its biosecurity law for prohibited goods when they declare all relevant goods before inspection, even if an item cannot enter the country.

What Happens With Customs on a Connecting Flight?

Connections are one of the most confusing parts of international arrival.

The answer depends on the country, airport, and baggage system.

For travelers entering the United States from overseas, CBP currently states that passengers normally have to collect their checked luggage and bring it through U.S. Customs and Border Protection at the first U.S. entry point.

After completing customs, travelers connecting onward may need to recheck the baggage.

Because this is airport and route specific, check your airline's current baggage instructions.

What Is U.S. Preclearance?

U.S. Preclearance changes where you complete immigration and customs.

Instead of waiting until you land in the United States, CBP officers at certain foreign airports inspect travelers before they board the U.S.-bound flight.

CBP personnel at preclearance locations carry out immigration, customs, and agriculture checks before departure.

What Happens After You Land in the U.S.?

A traveler who successfully completes U.S. Preclearance can generally arrive more like a domestic passenger and proceed directly to the connecting flight or destination rather than joining the normal CBP arrival line.

This can make a U.S. connection easier.

Do Not Confuse Preclearance With Normal Airline Check-In

Preclearance is an actual U.S. border inspection carried out outside the United States.

What Is Mobile Passport Control?

Mobile Passport Control, or MPC, is a free CBP service that allows eligible travelers entering the United States to submit travel document information, a photo, and arrival information through the official app.

MPC does not replace your passport.

It is a processing option that can make the CBP arrival process easier at participating locations.

As of 2026, CBP lists MPC at many major U.S. international airports and has also started offering it for eligible travelers at selected land-border pedestrian crossings.

Is Mobile Passport Control the Same as Global Entry?

No.

MPC and Global Entry are different programs.

MPC is a free processing option available to eligible travelers at participating locations.

Global Entry is a Trusted Traveler Program for pre-approved, low-risk travelers and currently has a $120 application fee. Applicants undergo a background check and approval process.

You do not need Global Entry membership simply to use MPC if you otherwise meet MPC eligibility rules.

Can Families Use Mobile Passport Control Together?

MPC supports group submissions for eligible travelers traveling together. CBP's current app process lets travelers create profiles and submit arrival information through one mobile device, subject to current MPC rules.

Families should still make sure every traveler has the proper passport and entry permission.

A parent's eligibility does not remove a child's passport or immigration requirements.

What Happens When You Return to the United States?

Returning home still involves border checks.

At your first U.S. port of entry, U.S. Customs and Border Protection may check your identity, travel documents, customs declaration, purchases, agricultural products, and other items you are bringing back.

CBP's current guidance says travelers make required declarations during the arrival process, which may be done orally to CBP officers or through an approved CBP mobile process depending on the port and traveler.

For many travelers arriving by air, the general process is:

Arrival → p assport/CBP inspection → baggage claim → customs → connecting flight or airport exit

U.S. Preclearance locations are different because the CBP inspection takes place before boarding the flight to the United States.

Whether an item can actually enter depends on what it is and where it came from.

A packaged product being sold legally overseas does not automatically mean it can enter the United States.

Check before packing it.

What Happens If Immigration Refuses Entry?

Foreign visitors can be refused admission when border authorities decide that they do not meet the country's entry requirements.

In the United States, CBP officers inspect people seeking admission and decide whether they are admissible under U.S. law.

Possible issues can involve:

  • ✓
    Incorrect travel documents
  • ✓
    Entry permission problems
  • ✓
    Travel purpose not matching the visa or status
  • ✓
    Immigration history
  • ✓
    Other legal grounds affecting admission

A visa does not itself guarantee entry into the United States.

What Happens Next?

The exact process depends on the country and circumstances.

A traveler may face:

  • ✓
    More questioning
  • ✓
    Secondary inspection
  • ✓
    Review of documents
  • ✓
    Withdrawal or refusal of entry where allowed
  • ✓
    Return travel
  • ✓
    Other immigration procedures

Do not assume that every secondary inspection ends in refusal.

CBP separately recognizes situations where travelers may be delayed, denied entry, or repeatedly sent for extra screening.

Can a U.S. Citizen Be Refused Entry to the United States?

A U.S. citizen returning home is in a different legal position from a foreign visitor seeking admission.

CBP can still inspect a U.S. citizen, verify identity and citizenship, inspect belongings, and send the traveler to additional screening.

So returning citizenship does not mean:

No border inspection.

It means the normal foreign-visitor admissibility process is not the same situation.

Carry a valid U.S. passport and cooperate with the normal inspection process.

What Is Secondary Inspection Really Like?

Secondary inspection means the first officer needs additional checking before the border process can be completed.

Possible reasons can include:

  • ✓
    Document verification
  • ✓
    Immigration-status questions
  • ✓
    Customs issues
  • ✓
    Baggage questions
  • ✓
    Identity checks
  • ✓
    Additional database checks

CBP distinguishes secondary inspection from other searches and notes that additional screening does not automatically involve searching electronic devices.

Being sent to secondary inspection does not by itself prove that you did anything wrong.

Follow instructions and answer accurately.

You should be able to explain basic facts such as:

  • ✓

  • ✓

  • ✓
    What your visa or travel status allows

Keep answers accurate and simple.

Do not invent details because you think they sound better.

Common Immigration Mistakes International Travelers Make

Many border problems begin with small planning errors.

1. Keeping the Passport in Checked Luggage

Your passport belongs in your carry-on or personal bag.

You normally need it before reaching baggage claim.

2. Not Knowing Your Accommodation Address

Save your first hotel or host address offline.

Do not depend completely on airport Wi-Fi.

3. Giving Answers That Conflict With Your Visa

If your visa is for tourism, your explanation of the trip should match your real tourist plans.

4. Forgetting the Onward Ticket

If your destination requires proof of departure, keep it easy to open.

5. Ignoring Transit Immigration

A connecting country can become an entry country if you must pass through immigration.

6. Using Old Entry Information

The State Department tells U.S. travelers to review current entry requirements and destination information before travel.

Check again near departure.

7. Making Jokes About Security or Illegal Items

Airport and border areas are not good places for jokes about weapons, drugs, false documents, or security threats.

Answer questions normally.

Common Customs Mistakes Travelers Make

Customs mistakes often happen because travelers think only obviously illegal goods matter.

1. Assuming Packaged Food Is Automatically Allowed

Food rules can depend on ingredients and country of origin.

Check before packing.

2. Not Declaring Gifts

A gift can still count as an item acquired abroad.

3. Confusing Duty-Free With No Declaration

Buying an item duty-free does not remove the destination country's import rules.

4. Hiding an Item Because You Are Unsure

If the rules require a declaration, hiding the item can make the situation worse.

5. Forgetting Medicines

Medicines can have separate customs and controlled-substance rules.

6. Ignoring Cash Reporting Rules

Some countries require travelers carrying more than a stated amount of currency or monetary instruments to make a declaration.

7. Throwing Away Receipts Too Early

Receipts can help show the value of expensive purchases.

8. Assuming Every Country Has the Same Allowance

Customs limits vary by country.

A U.S. allowance does not tell you what you may take into the UK, Australia, Japan, or another destination.

Final Thoughts

In short, Customs and immigration become much easier once you stop treating them as one process.

Immigration deals with you. Customs deals with what you carry.

At immigration, have your passport, entry permission, accommodation information, and other required travel documents ready.

At customs, think about purchases, gifts, food, medicines, alcohol, tobacco, cash, and restricted items.

I prefer checking both before packing. It is much easier to leave a restricted item at home or prepare the correct document than to discover the rule after a long international flight.

The final rule is simple: use current official immigration and customs information, answer border questions accurately, and declare items when the destination requires it. That preparation can make your first international arrival far less stressful.
