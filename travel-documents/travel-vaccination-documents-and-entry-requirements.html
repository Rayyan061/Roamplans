Travel Vaccination Documents and Entry Requirements

✦ Vaccination proof✓ Yellow Card & ICVP↕ Route-based entry rules

Travel vaccines can affect an international trip in two different ways ↗. A vaccine may be recommended to protect your health, or a country may require official proof of vaccination before allowing you to enter. Those are not the same thing.

That difference matters because travelers sometimes see a long list of recommended vaccines and assume they must carry proof of every one at immigration. In many cases, they do not. On the other hand, certain destinations may require an official vaccination certificate for a specific disease, especially yellow fever, based on where you are arriving from or where you recently traveled. CDC destination pages separate health recommendations from country entry requirements for this reason.

For travel planning, I would check health-document rules at the same time as passport and visa requirements. Your nationality, destination, age, travel route, recent countries visited, and even a long airport transit can affect what proof may be required. CDC warns that some yellow fever certificate rules can apply after travel through a risk country, including certain layovers.

This guide focuses on travel vaccination documents and entry requirements. It explains which vaccination records may matter at the border, how the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis works, why your route can change the requirement, and where to check current rules before departure.

!
Travel Note

Health and vaccination entry rules can change. Check the latest information from CDC, the destination government, its embassy or consulate, and other official health authorities before traveling. The U.S. Department of State also advises travelers to confirm current vaccine and medical-document requirements for their destination.

Recommended Vaccines Vs Required Vaccines

This is the first distinction every traveler should know.

Recommended Vaccination

A health authority may recommend a vaccine because it can reduce your risk of getting sick during the trip.

CDC destination guidance can recommend vaccines based on factors such as:

  • ✓
    Destination
  • ✓
    Disease risk
  • ✓
    Age
  • ✓
    Health situation
  • ✓
    Activities
  • ✓
    Length of stay
  • ✓
    Areas you plan to visit

CDC also advises international travelers to be up to date on routine vaccines and currently recommends that travelers be fully vaccinated against measles before international travel.

A recommendation does not automatically mean an immigration officer will ask to see proof.

Required Vaccination

A required vaccination is different.

The destination government may make proof of a specific vaccination a legal entry condition for certain travelers.

The requirement might apply to:

  • ✓
    Everyone arriving
  • ✓
    Travelers above or below a certain age
  • ✓
    Travelers coming from a country with disease risk
  • ✓
    Travelers who recently visited a risk country
  • ✓
    Travelers who transited through certain countries

Yellow fever is the clearest example. WHO says countries may require proof of yellow fever vaccination from travelers under the International Health Regulations.

So always separate these questions:

What vaccines are recommended for my health?

and

What vaccination proof is required for entry?

They can produce different answers.

What Is a Travel Vaccination Certificate?

A travel vaccination certificate is an official record showing that a traveler received a vaccine or another approved form of prophylaxis.

Depending on the requirement, the record may include:

  • ✓
    Traveler's full name
  • ✓
    Date of birth
  • ✓
    Vaccine received
  • ✓
    Date given
  • ✓
    Vaccine manufacturer or batch information
  • ✓
    Healthcare professional's signature
  • ✓
    Clinic or official stamp
  • ✓
    Certificate-validity period

The document format matters where an international rule requires a particular certificate.

A normal pharmacy receipt, patient portal screenshot, or handwritten note may show that you received a vaccine but may not satisfy a border requirement that calls for an official international certificate.

What Is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis?

The International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis, usually shortened to ICVP, is an internationally recognized health document used under the International Health Regulations.

Travelers often call it the:

Yellow Card

or

yellow fever certificate.

CDC says yellow fever vaccination for international entry purposes must currently be documented using the ICVP.

The certificate is more than a simple vaccination record.

For yellow fever, it must be completed according to official requirements, including the vaccination information, provider signature, and validation from an approved yellow fever vaccination center. CDC says the provider must use the center's official uniform stamp, and a signature stamp is not accepted in place of the provider's handwritten signature.

Is the Yellow Card Only for Yellow Fever?

The ICVP is most strongly associated with yellow fever because that is the vaccination most commonly documented under current international entry rules.

CDC notes that the booklet can also contain records of other vaccinations or prophylaxis where relevant, but yellow fever has the established International Health Regulations certificate requirements used by many countries.

Do not assume that every vaccine you receive needs to be entered on an ICVP.

Check what the destination actually requires.

When Is Yellow Fever Vaccination Proof Required?

There is no single rule for every destination.

A country may require proof of yellow fever vaccination in several ways.

Requirement for All Arriving Travelers

Some countries require proof from nearly all travelers who meet the applicable age conditions.

CDC currently lists a group of destinations that require proof of yellow fever vaccination from all arriving travelers, subject to country-specific details and changing rules.

Requirement Based on Where You Are Arriving From

Other destinations require the certificate only if you arrive from a country where yellow fever transmission is considered a risk.

For example, CDC's current Costa Rica guidance says yellow fever vaccination is required for travelers aged 9 months or older arriving from countries with yellow fever risk.

If you fly directly from the United States, your requirement may therefore be different from someone arriving from another country.

Requirement Based on Recent Travel

Some governments also look at where you have been before the immediate flight.

That means a trip such as:

United States → Brazil → Destination Country

may have different vaccination-document requirements from:

United States → Destination Country

even when the final destination is exactly the same.

CDC's yellow fever country guidance bases many requirements on arrival from or travel through countries with yellow fever transmission risk.

Can a Layover Trigger a Yellow Fever Certificate Requirement?

Yes, in some cases.

This is one of the easier travel-document rules to miss.

CDC specifically warns that some countries require proof of yellow fever vaccination from travelers arriving from a risk country even after a brief layover, depending on the destination's rule.

Other countries may use a particular transit-time threshold.

This means you should not check only:

Where does my trip start?

Also check:

  • ✓
    Every connecting country
  • ✓
    Length of each layover
  • ✓
    Whether you leave the airport
  • ✓
    Whether the destination counts airport transit
  • ✓
    Recent travel before the trip

A connection that seems like a simple flight change can affect your health-document requirement.

A Route Can Change the Requirement Even When Your Destination Does Not

Consider two travelers.

Traveler A

New York → Destination

No travel through a yellow fever risk country.

Traveler B

New York → Yellow-fever-risk country → Destination

Traveler B may face a vaccination-certificate requirement that Traveler A does not.

CDC's India guidance gives a clear example. Yellow fever vaccination is not required for a traveler going directly from the United States, but India applies yellow fever rules to certain travelers arriving after being in countries with yellow fever risk.

This is why a country entry page needs to be read together with the full itinerary.

How Long Before Travel Should You Get a Yellow Fever Vaccine?

If you need yellow fever vaccination proof for entry, timing matters.

CDC states that a properly completed ICVP becomes valid 10 days after the date of the primary yellow fever vaccination.

So if you receive your first yellow fever vaccination only a day or two before reaching a country that requires a valid certificate, the certificate may not yet meet the entry rule.

Do not leave this check until the final week.

Example

Suppose you receive your first yellow fever vaccination on:

June 1

Under current ICVP rules, the certificate becomes valid:

June 11

That timing can affect when you are permitted to enter a country requiring valid proof.

For health reasons and personal vaccine advice, travelers should discuss their trip with an appropriate healthcare professional rather than using an entry deadline alone to decide when a vaccine should be given.

How Long Is a Yellow Fever Certificate Valid?

This is another area where old travel information can mislead people.

In the past, travelers often heard that yellow fever certificates were valid for 10 years.

That rule changed.

Under the International Health Regulations, the protection period and validity of a properly completed yellow fever vaccination certificate are now for the lifetime of the vaccinated person. WHO's updated IHR rules replaced the previous 10-year certificate limit.

CDC confirms that, since the 2016 change, a properly completed ICVP is considered valid for life, whether the vaccination occurred before or after that rule change.

Does That Mean Nobody Ever Needs Another Yellow Fever Dose?

Not necessarily.

The certificate rule and a healthcare professional's vaccine recommendation are separate questions.

CDC states that one dose is adequate for most travelers, but certain people or higher-risk travel situations may have medical reasons for an additional dose.

That is a health decision to discuss with a qualified healthcare professional.

For border-document planning, the key point is:

Countries cannot require routine yellow fever revaccination every 10 years simply because an older ICVP passed the former 10-year period.

Where Can U.S. Travelers Get an Official Yellow Fever Certificate?

Not every clinic can issue a valid yellow fever ICVP.

CDC says yellow fever vaccination for U.S. travelers must be provided through a designated yellow fever vaccination center that has the official uniform stamp needed to validate the certificate.

CDC provides a Yellow Fever Vaccination Clinic search tool for finding approved locations.

When making the appointment, confirm that the clinic can:

  • ✓
    Give the yellow fever vaccine where medically appropriate
  • ✓
    Complete the ICVP
  • ✓
    Sign it correctly
  • ✓
    Apply the official validation stamp

Do not assume any pharmacy or travel clinic automatically has authority to issue the certificate.

Make Sure the Name on the Certificate Matches Your Passport

CDC's ICVP instructions say the traveler's name should be written exactly as it appears in the passport.

This is a simple check but worth doing before leaving the clinic.

Compare:

  • ✓
    Full name
  • ✓
    Date of birth
  • ✓
    Vaccine date
  • ✓
    Certificate-validity information
  • ✓
    Provider signature
  • ✓
    Official stamp

If anything looks wrong or incomplete, ask the clinic before traveling.

A border is a poor place to discover that an official document was filled out incorrectly.

Should You Carry the Original Yellow Card?

If your destination requires an ICVP, plan to carry the official certificate itself.

CDC describes the ICVP as the document used to prove yellow fever vaccination for entry.

A phone photo is still useful as a backup, but I would not treat it as a guaranteed replacement for the original certificate.

Keep the Yellow Card:

  • ✓
    With your passport or travel-document folder
  • ✓
    In your carry-on
  • ✓
    Protected from water or damage
  • ✓
    Easy to reach during airline and immigration checks

Do not place the only original inside checked luggage.

Can an Airline Check Your Vaccination Certificate?

It can.

Airlines may check destination health and entry documents before boarding because a passenger must meet the rules for the route.

The U.S. Department of State advises travelers that they may need to carry an International Certificate of Vaccination or other proof of vaccinations or medical tests and recommends checking foreign embassy requirements.

That means you should have any required health certificate available before boarding, not just after landing.

What Happens If You Arrive Without Required Yellow Fever Proof?

The outcome depends on the destination.

CDC states that travelers who arrive at a country requiring yellow fever documentation without a valid ICVP or accepted medical waiver could face outcomes such as:

  • ✓
    Denied entry
  • ✓
    Quarantine for up to six days
  • ✓
    Vaccination at the point of entry

depending on the country's rules.

That is why this document deserves the same planning attention as a passport or visa when it applies.

Do not rely on:

They probably won't ask.

If the certificate is an entry requirement, prepare it properly.

What If You Cannot Receive the Yellow Fever Vaccine for Medical Reasons?

Some travelers may have a medical condition or other clinical reason that makes yellow fever vaccination inappropriate.

CDC allows authorized yellow fever vaccine providers to issue a medical waiver when a traveler has a recognized contraindication or precaution and the healthcare professional determines vaccination should not be given.

The waiver process can include:

  • ✓
    Completion of the medical contraindication section of the ICVP
  • ✓
    Signed and dated waiver letter
  • ✓
    Official yellow fever vaccination-center stamp
  • ✓
    Explanation of the medical reason

A Medical Waiver Does Not Guarantee Entry

This point matters.

CDC warns that the destination country may decide not to accept a medical waiver. It recommends contacting the embassy or consulate before travel to confirm how the destination treats the exemption.

If you rely on a waiver, check the rule well before booking a difficult-to-change trip.

Do Not Seek a Medical Waiver Just to Avoid the Vaccine

CDC's current guidance says reasons other than recognized medical contraindications or precautions are not acceptable grounds for a yellow fever vaccination exemption under this process.

The decision should come from an authorized healthcare professional based on medical factors.

This article can explain the travel-document process, but personal vaccination decisions should be discussed with a qualified clinician who can review your health and itinerary.

Polio Vaccination Documents for International Travel

Polio can create a travel-document requirement in certain situations.

CDC says travelers should be fully vaccinated against polio before international travel. It also warns that governments in countries with increased poliovirus risk may require some travelers to show proof of polio vaccination when departing.

The requirement can depend on:

  • ✓
    Country
  • ✓
    Current polio situation
  • ✓
    Length of stay
  • ✓
    Traveler age
  • ✓
    Vaccination history
  • ✓
    Departure route

This means a traveler may enter a country without being asked for polio documentation but later face a vaccination-proof rule when leaving.

Long-Term Travelers Need Extra Attention

Under current international polio guidance, residents and certain long-term visitors in affected countries can be advised or required to receive a polio dose within a defined period before international departure. WHO guidance has used a window of 4 weeks to 12 months before travel for affected groups.

CDC also notes that long-term travelers and residents in certain countries can be required to show proof of polio vaccination when departing.

Do not assume the same rule applies to every short tourist.

Check the current country-specific requirement.

How Is Polio Vaccination Documented for Travel?

When official international proof is required, polio vaccination can be recorded on the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis.

CDC specifically states that governments may require proof of polio vaccination on the yellow ICVP when travelers leave certain higher-risk countries.

This is useful because the Yellow Card is not limited to recording yellow fever.

It can also be used for other vaccination documentation under international travel-health rules where appropriate.

If your itinerary could trigger a polio requirement, ask your travel-health provider whether the dose needs to be recorded on an ICVP.

Do You Need Polio Proof for Every International Trip?

No.

Polio vaccination recommendations and border-document requirements are different.

For example, CDC may recommend vaccination or a booster because poliovirus has recently been detected in a destination, but that does not automatically mean the destination requires every arriving tourist to present a polio certificate.

Always separate:

Health recommendation

from

Entry or exit document requirement.

That same distinction applies throughout this guide.

Meningococcal Vaccination Certificates

Meningococcal vaccination is another example where proof can become part of a specific type of international trip.

Saudi Arabia uses vaccination requirements for travelers going for Hajj and Umrah.

For the 2026 Hajj season, Saudi Ministry of Health guidance required meningococcal vaccination for covered pilgrims and set timing and validity conditions for the certificate.

This is a good example of why travel purpose matters.

A person entering a country for a normal tourist visit may face different health-document rules from someone entering for:

  • ✓

  • ✓
    Umrah
  • ✓
    A mass gathering
  • ✓
    Another regulated event

Do not assume ordinary tourist entry rules cover a religious pilgrimage or large international event.

What If You Lose Your Yellow Card?

A lost Yellow Card does not always mean you must immediately receive another yellow fever vaccination.

CDC provides a process for reissuing an ICVP when the original has been lost, damaged, or needs updating after a name change.

An authorized clinician may reissue the certificate if the original vaccination information can be confirmed.

CDC says verification should include information such as:

  • ✓
    Traveler's name
  • ✓
    Date of birth
  • ✓
    Vaccination date
  • ✓
    Vaccine manufacturer
  • ✓
    Batch or lot number
  • ✓
    Original signature and stamp information where available

This is one reason keeping a clear copy of the original certificate can be very useful.

How Is a Replacement ICVP Completed?

When an authorized clinician can verify the original vaccination, CDC says the replacement certificate should record the original vaccination date, not the date the new card was issued.

For yellow fever, the replacement should also include the correct vaccine details, clinician signature, and official stamp.

The validity is based on the original vaccination.

A properly documented yellow fever certificate remains valid for the lifetime of the vaccinated person under current international rules.

Start With the Clinic That Gave the Vaccine

If your ICVP is lost, I would contact the original vaccination center first.

They may have the records needed to confirm:

  • ✓
    Vaccination date
  • ✓
    Vaccine product
  • ✓
    Lot number
  • ✓
    Your identity
  • ✓
    Original certificate information

If that clinic cannot help, contact another authorized yellow fever vaccination center and explain what records you have.

CDC provides a U.S. search tool for authorized yellow fever vaccination centers.

Do this well before departure because rebuilding old vaccination documentation can take time.

What If You Cannot Find Your Old Vaccination Records?

    Pharmacy

  • ✓

Should You Get Vaccinated Again If Records Are Missing?

That is a medical decision rather than a travel-document decision.

CDC says that when routine vaccination records cannot be found, some vaccines may need to be given again, and blood testing can sometimes be used to check immunity for certain diseases.

For a child, CDC also notes that repeating some vaccinations can be used when records cannot be found, but parents should discuss the situation with the child's healthcare professional.

Do not repeat a travel vaccine solely because a website told you that losing paperwork means another dose is required.

Speak with a qualified clinician.

Travel Insurance Does Not Replace Vaccination Documents

Travel insurance and vaccination certificates serve different purposes.

Travel insurance may help with medical costs, trip disruption, evacuation, or other covered events. A vaccination certificate proves that you meet a health-document rule when a destination requires one.

Having travel insurance does not replace:

  • ✓
    International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis
  • ✓
    Required yellow fever proof
  • ✓
    Required polio documentation
  • ✓
    Accepted medical waiver
  • ✓
    Other health records specifically required for entry

The U.S. Department of State advises travelers to check whether they need an International Certificate of Vaccination or proof of other vaccinations or medical tests for their destination.

Treat these as separate parts of your travel file:

Insurance documents = financial and assistance information

Vaccination documents = health and entry evidence

Can Airlines Check Vaccination Documents Before Boarding?

Yes, when proof of vaccination or another health document is part of the destination's entry rules.

Do not assume the first check will happen after landing.

If the destination requires an ICVP or another health document, keep it with your passport and other entry paperwork during check-in and boarding.

The State Department specifically says travelers may need to carry a Yellow Card or proof of other vaccinations or medical tests and recommends checking current requirements with foreign embassies.

For yellow fever, CDC says some countries require travelers to show an ICVP as a condition of entry, including in cases connected to previous travel or transit through yellow-fever-risk countries.

Keep Required Health Documents in Your Carry-On

What Happens at the Border With a Vaccination Certificate?

The process depends on the country.

You may never be asked for a vaccination record if your route does not trigger a requirement.

If proof is required, an official may check:

  • ✓

For yellow fever, CDC states that the ICVP must be properly completed and validated to serve as official proof.

Make sure the certificate belongs to the traveler whose passport is being presented.

Your Travel Route Can Matter at the Border

Health-document checks are one reason travelers should keep information about their recent route available.

A destination may care about more than the country printed on your passport.

For yellow fever, some entry rules apply based on whether a traveler has been in or transited through a country with yellow fever risk. CDC notes that even a layover can matter under some country rules.

That means immigration or health officials may need to know:

  • ✓
    Where your trip started
  • ✓
    Countries recently visited
  • ✓
    Transit airports
  • ✓
    Length of layovers
  • ✓
    Whether you left the airport
  • ✓
    When you were in a risk area

Your flight itinerary can therefore be useful alongside your vaccination certificate.

What If Your Yellow Fever Certificate Is Less Than 10 Days Old?

For a first yellow fever vaccination, the ICVP does not become valid for international entry purposes immediately.

Under current international rules, validity begins 10 days after the primary vaccination. CDC and WHO both confirm this timing.

For example:

Vaccinated: August 1

Certificate valid from: August 11

If you reach a destination requiring valid proof on August 5, simply carrying the certificate does not mean the timing requirement has been met.

This is why travelers should check vaccination-document rules well before departure.

Does a Yellow Fever Certificate Expire After 10 Years?

No, not under the current international certificate rule.

WHO changed the International Health Regulations so that a valid yellow fever vaccination certificate lasts for the lifetime of the vaccinated person, beginning 10 days after the primary vaccination.

CDC follows the same rule and explains that properly completed ICVPs are valid for life.

This applies even though older Yellow Cards may show wording based on the former 10-year rule.

Do not get another vaccine simply because an old blog post says the certificate expires every 10 years. Any medical decision about an additional dose should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

What If a Country Still Asks for a Yellow Fever Booster?

Check the current official rule before taking action.

The internationally recognized certificate rule provides lifetime validity.

If you find a government page that appears to demand routine revaccination because the original dose is more than 10 years old, compare it with:

  • ✓
    Current destination government information
  • ✓
    CDC destination guidance
  • ✓
    WHO information
  • ✓
    The destination's embassy or consulate

If the information still appears inconsistent, contact the authority responsible for entry.

Do not make a medical decision based on an outdated travel page.

Can a Medical Waiver Be Checked at the Border?

Yes.

A traveler who cannot receive yellow fever vaccine for medical reasons may be given a medical waiver by an authorized provider.

CDC says the waiver should include the medical contraindication section of the ICVP and supporting documentation from the healthcare professional. However, a foreign government is not required to accept the waiver.

If you are relying on a waiver:

  • ✓
    Carry the original documents
  • ✓
    Keep a backup copy
  • ✓
    Confirm acceptance before travel
  • ✓
    Contact the destination embassy where needed
  • ✓
    Check transit-country rules too

Do this before paying for difficult-to-change travel.

What If a Destination Does Not Accept Your Medical Waiver?

The destination government controls its own entry decision.

CDC warns that countries may decline to accept a yellow fever medical waiver.

If the destination confirms that the waiver will not satisfy its entry rule, discuss your options with your healthcare professional and reconsider the itinerary if necessary.

Possible travel-planning changes may include:

  • ✓
    Different route
  • ✓
    Different transit country
  • ✓
    Different destination
  • ✓
    Different travel date

Do not assume an airline can override the destination government's health requirement.

What If You Forgot Your Yellow Card at Home?

If proof is required, do not assume a phone photo will be accepted in place of the official certificate.

CDC identifies the ICVP as the official document used to prove yellow fever vaccination.

If you discover the problem before departure:

  • ✓

  • ✓

A secure scan is useful because it can help the clinic identify the original record, but the image itself should not be treated as guaranteed border proof.

Do not wait until immigration to find out whether the document can be read.

What If Your Name Changed After Vaccination?

A name change can leave your old vaccination certificate showing different personal information from your current passport.

CDC includes name changes among the situations where an ICVP may need to be reissued.

If this applies to you, contact an authorized yellow fever vaccination center before travel.

Bring any records needed to connect:

  • ✓
    Previous name
  • ✓
    Current name
  • ✓
    Vaccination history
  • ✓
    Passport identity

The goal is to have travel health records that can be clearly linked to you.

Common Travel Vaccination Document Mistakes

Most problems are easier to fix several weeks before travel than at an airline counter.

1. Confusing Recommended Vaccines With Entry Requirements

CDC destination pages distinguish between vaccines recommended for health protection and vaccines required for entry.

Check both sections.

2. Checking Only the Final Destination

Yellow fever rules can depend on travel through or arrival from another country.

Check the full route.

3. Ignoring Airport Layovers

Some destinations count transit through a yellow-fever-risk country when deciding whether proof is required.

Read the destination's specific transit rule.

4. Getting Yellow Fever Vaccine Too Late

A primary yellow fever certificate becomes valid 10 days after vaccination.

Do not schedule the first dose immediately before a trip that requires valid proof.

5. Believing the Yellow Card Expires Every 10 Years

Current international rules provide lifetime certificate validity.

Old travel advice may still mention the former rule.

6. Carrying Only a Phone Screenshot

A screenshot is a useful backup, but where yellow fever proof is required, the ICVP is the recognized certificate.

Carry the original.

7. Putting the Certificate in Checked Baggage

A required health document can be needed before or during border processing.

Keep it with your passport.

8. Forgetting to Check the Name

CDC's ICVP guidance requires the traveler's identity information to be properly recorded.

Check it before leaving the clinic.

9. Losing an Old Yellow Card

A yellow fever ICVP can remain valid for life, so an old certificate may still be very useful.

Store it carefully and keep a secure copy.

10. Assuming All Clinics Can Issue the Official Certificate

Yellow fever travel documentation must be handled through authorized vaccination centers that can properly validate the ICVP.

Confirm this before your appointment.

11. Forgetting Polio Departure Requirements

CDC warns that certain countries with increased poliovirus risk may require travelers to show proof of polio vaccination on the ICVP when leaving.

This can matter even when arrival was straightforward.

12. Ignoring Special-Trip Requirements

Pilgrimages, mass gatherings, and other specific travel purposes can have health requirements beyond normal tourism rules.

Check the official requirements linked to the event as well as general destination information.

13. Depending on an Old Country List

Health requirements can change with disease activity and government rules.

CDC maintains destination-specific travel health information and current yellow fever guidance for this reason.

Check again before departure.

Final Thoughts

In short, Travel vaccination documents are much easier to manage once you separate health advice from border requirements.

A vaccine may be recommended because it can protect you during the trip without being required at immigration. Another vaccine may come with an official certificate that must be shown because your destination, route, or recent travel triggers an entry rule.

Yellow fever is the main example. Its ICVP can be required based on where you are arriving from or where you transited, and a properly completed certificate is currently valid for life after the initial 10-day waiting period.

Polio and special travel situations can create other documentation needs as well. CDC notes that some countries with increased poliovirus risk can require proof when travelers leave.

My planning rule is simple: check the destination, check the entire route, check the document format, and check again before departure.

For personal vaccination decisions, use a qualified healthcare professional. For border rules, use current government, CDC, WHO, embassy, and immigration information. That keeps medical advice and travel-document requirements in their proper roles.
