What to Pack for Japan: First-Time Visitor Packing List

Use this first-time Japan packing list to choose seasonal clothing, shoes, travel documents, electronics, toiletries, medication, and luggage.
First-time checklistSeasonal layersLuggage strategy

Packing for Japan is less about bringing more things and more about bringing the right things. First-time visitors often prepare for every possible situation, then discover that a heavy suitcase is harder to manage through train stations, hotel changes, stairs, and crowded city transport than they expected.

When I build a Japan packing list, I start with the trip itself: season, cities, number of hotel changes, expected walking, luggage size, and anything that cannot easily be replaced after arrival. Japan has convenience stores, pharmacies, clothing shops, electronics stores, and laundry options in major travel areas, so forgetting an ordinary toiletry is usually less serious than forgetting medication documents or packing shoes that hurt after one full sightseeing day.

This packing guide is built for a typical first visit to Japan, especially a Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka trip. Seasonal clothing gets its own section later, but the first goal is to make sure your core luggage is practical before adding weather-specific items.

Start by Packing Lighter Than You Think

If your trip includes Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and several train journeys, luggage mobility matters.

Large railway stations can involve:

  • ✓
    Long corridors
  • ✓
    Stairs
  • ✓
    Escalators
  • ✓
    Elevators with queues
  • ✓
    Platform changes
  • ✓
    Crowded trains
  • ✓
    Walking between station exits and hotels

That is why I would rather pack a medium suitcase that I can control myself than fill the largest suitcase my airline allows.

Japan's tourism authority also recommends lighter travel where possible and points travelers toward luggage forwarding services for larger bags.

Before choosing your suitcase, ask:

Can I lift this bag onto a luggage rack or move it through a busy station without help?

If the answer is no, either reduce the contents or plan how you will move the bag between cities.

Large Luggage Can Affect Shinkansen Travel

This is one Japan-specific packing issue worth checking before departure.

On the Tokaido, Sanyo, and Kyushu Shinkansen routes, luggage with combined dimensions above 160 cm and up to 250 cm may require a reservation for an appropriate oversized baggage space. JR Central says travelers without the required reservation may be charged a ¥1,000 baggage fee.

You do not need to memorize every measurement while packing.

Just measure a large suitcase before the trip if you expect to use the Shinkansen.

If your bag is oversized, make the transport plan before the day you travel.

Check Medication Before Putting It in Your Bag

This deserves more attention than it gets on generic packing lists.

Japan has rules for bringing medicines into the country. The Ministry of Health states that many general prescription medicines can be brought for personal use without a special procedure when the amount is up to one month's supply, but prohibited and specially controlled substances have separate rules. Larger quantities may require an Import Confirmation before travel.

So do not assume:

I have a prescription in the U.S., so I can automatically carry it into Japan.

Before packing regular medication:

  • ✓
    Check the active ingredient.
  • ✓
    Check Japan's current official rules.
  • ✓
    Check the quantity you are carrying.
  • ✓
    Complete any required paperwork early.
  • ✓
    Keep medication in clearly identifiable packaging where practical.

This is one item I would verify before buying a non-refundable flight.

A shoe that feels fine during a short walk at home may become uncomfortable after several full city days.

Pack shoes you have already worn.

A Japan trip is a poor time to break in a brand-new pair.

Should You Bring Two Pairs of Shoes?

Usually, I would bring:

one dependable main walking pair

and possibly:

one light second pair

The second pair can help if the first gets wet or you want something different for evenings.

But I would not bring three or four pairs just to match different outfits.

Shoes use a large amount of luggage space.

For most first-time city trips, comfort matters more than variety.

Easy-to-Remove Shoes Can Be Useful

There are places in Japan where shoes may need to be removed, including some traditional accommodation, temples, homes, and certain restaurants.

You do not need special Japanese travel shoes.

But a comfortable pair that is easy to put on and remove can make those situations simpler.

Also pack socks you are comfortable wearing when your shoes come off.

Use Layers Instead of Packing for Every Temperature

Spring and autumn can include cool mornings, warmer afternoons, and colder evenings.

Rather than bringing separate outfits for every temperature, I prefer clothing that works in layers.

A simple system might be:

base clothing → light layer → weather layer

That gives you more flexibility without filling the suitcase.

Do You Need a Plug Adapter in Japan?

Japan mainly uses Type A plugs, with two flat parallel pins, which is the same basic plug shape used in the United States.

But voltage matters too.

Japan uses around 100 volts, while U.S. electricity is normally around 120 volts.

Many modern phone, laptop, and camera chargers are rated for a wide voltage range such as:

100–240V

If your charger shows that range, it will usually work without a voltage converter.

Check the label on each device before travel.

For high-powered appliances such as hair tools, do not assume compatibility just because the plug fits.

Bring Personal Products That May Be Hard to Replace

A specific skin product, prescription cream, specialty contact-lens product, or allergy-safe item may be harder.

This is how I decide:

Can I replace this easily with any equivalent?

If yes, it is lower priority.

Would I need this exact product?

If yes, pack enough for the trip.

That method prevents a toiletry bag from becoming half the suitcase.

Keep cash and cards in separate places so losing one wallet does not remove every payment option.

Do You Need an IC Card Before Packing?

No physical IC card needs to be packed from home.

Cards such as Suica, PASMO, and ICOCA are mainly used after arrival for compatible trains, subways, buses, and some everyday purchases.

The full setup belongs in IC Cards in Japan: Suica, PASMO and ICOCA Explained.

For packing purposes, you mainly need a working phone and payment method.

Final Thoughts

In short, A good Japan packing list should make the trip easier, not make your suitcase heavier. Pack around the season, your walking needs, and how often you change hotels. Bring the personal items that are difficult to replace, plan laundry for longer trips, and leave enough room to move comfortably through Japan's stations and bring a few things home.
